2. The gnomon

The style (the edge that casts the time-telling shadow) must point at the celestial pole, so its angle above the dial plate equals your latitude. A dial sold with a fixed gnomon only reads true at one latitude.

3. Hour lines

Morning lines fall on the west side of the noon line, afternoon on the east (layout reverses in the southern hemisphere). Angles use the published formula for your dial type. Hours within 6 of noon are the reliable reading band on most days.

5. Reading the shadow against a clock

A flat dial reads apparent solar time. Your clock reads mean time on a timezone meridian: the equation of time (common published approximation, within about a minute) plus 4 minutes per degree of longitude separate them. Daylight saving shifts the answer one more hour - add it yourself when your clock is on DST.

Model: ideal gnomon with a knife-edge style, level plate, vertical wall exactly facing the equator. Real dials lose a few minutes to plate tilt, thick styles and reading the wrong edge. Hour-line formulas are standard published sundial construction; EoT is the common approximation, good to about a minute.
